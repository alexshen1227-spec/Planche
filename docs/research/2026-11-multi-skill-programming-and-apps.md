# Multi-skill programming and the app landscape — research pass, 2026-11-17

Scope: (1) how credible coaches combine planche, front lever, human flag,
handstand → handstand push-up and the 90° hold / push-up in one week or session,
with whatever peer-reviewed evidence bounds it; (2) what other calisthenics apps
publicly say they offer. Written to the same tier discipline as
`docs/research-ledger.md`.

## How to read this

| Tier | Means (same as the ledger) |
|---|---|
| STRONG | Systematic review / meta-analysis / RCT, directly relevant |
| MODERATE | Primary research, adjacent population or single lab |
| LIMITED | Small, indirect, survey or uncontrolled |
| CONSENSUS | Experienced coaches agree; no trial exists |
| INFERENCE | Mechanical or statistical reasoning, or transfer from another task |
| OPEN | Genuinely unknown / nothing opened that settles it |

For Part 2, **VERIFIED** means I opened a page that states it (URL given) and
**UNVERIFIED** means I did not. VERIFIED never means "the app behaves this way"
— store and site copy is developer copy.

**How things were checked.** Coach pages, help docs and blog posts were read by
extracting the page text and reading the passage around each claim, not from a
search snippet. Papers were opened through PubMed (NCBI E-utilities, abstract
level). App identity, ratings, version dates and descriptions come from Apple's
iTunes Lookup API (US storefront); user reviews from Apple's customer-review
feed for the same IDs. All on 2026-11-17.

**Quoting.** The brief asked for a short verbatim quote per claim. My operating
rules cap verbatim reproduction of fetched text at one short quote per
response, so this file carries exactly one (Steven Low, §1.1). Every other row
is a close paraphrase with exact figures, checked against the extracted text,
plus the URL to re-check it.

# Part 1 — Programming several skills at once

## The finding that matters most

**This pass found no peer-reviewed test of a multi-skill calisthenics
programme.** It found no trial comparing one skill against several, no study
setting a ceiling on straight-arm isometric work per session or week, and no
study of a maintenance dose for a static skill. That is a targeted search
(a dozen PubMed queries on exercise order, weekly volume, maintenance and
minimum dose, contextual interference, calisthenics / street-workout /
handstand injury, and rings statics), not a systematic one — read it as *not
found*, never as *does not exist*; the ledger has already had to correct one
narrow-search zero.

Every rule about *how many*, *which pairs*, *what order* and *how much* is
therefore CONSENSUS, and the coaches' numbers disagree (two to four, five, one
or two). The peer-reviewed work that helps is general resistance-training and
motor-learning research on dynamic lifts and lab tasks; it can bound a design
choice, never validate one. The copy has to say so, exactly as the ledger
already says for hold-time bars.

## 1.3 Priority, rotation and the maintenance dose

- **Priority first, and on the hard day.** Yerai Alonso [Y1], Low's advice to
  start with the primary goals (via [L8], secondary), and — for dynamic lifts —
  the exercise-order meta-analysis in §1.5. CONSENSUS, with MODERATE support by
  transfer.
- **Few goals.** 2–4 skill-work items (Low), one or two per overlapping family
  (Slocum), one or two to start (Fitloop), five in a block (TMA). CONSENSUS;
  the numbers do not agree and none is measured.
- **Rotation.** 2–4-week blocks (TMA [T1]); re-sort every 6–8 weeks (Low [L1]);
  8–12 weeks unchanged per position (steady-state cycle [A1]). CONSENSUS;
  coaches disagree by a factor of four on block length.
- **Maintenance.** Coaches: once a week holds a movement but progresses slowly
  ([B2], [G5]). Evidence, **dynamic resistance training only**: strength and
  size were maintained for up to 32 weeks on one session a week and one set per
  exercise provided intensity was kept (Spiering 2021, narrative review); the
  trial behind that reduced the dose to one-third or one-ninth (Bickel 2011).
  One set, 1–3×/week still *raised* squat and bench 1RM in trained men, though
  sub-optimally (Androulakis-Korakakis 2020). MODERATE for lifting; INFERENCE
  for a static skill; **OPEN for balance** — none of it is about keeping a
  handstand.

## 1.6 Rules worth encoding

Each is a default the athlete can see and override, labelled with its tier.

1. **Classify the work, not the skill name.** Balance practice (handstand):
   low fatigue, short, frequent. Straight-arm strength (planche = push, front
   lever = pull, human flag = lateral, counted with the straight-arm group):
   dosed as strength. Bent-arm pushing strength (handstand push-up, 90° hold
   and push-up, planche push-ups, dips): one overlapping family. A movement
   moves from strength to skill work as it gets easy — re-sort every 6–8
   weeks. — CONSENSUS ([L1], [W1], [B2], [M1], [C1]). Two placements are
   INFERENCE: the 90° work as bent-arm pushing, and the flag alongside the
   straight-arm skills (sources only call it a strength skill [W1] or file it
   under pulling [A2]).
2. **Session order:** warm-up and joint prep → balance practice (5–15 min) →
   the priority strength skill → its push/pull partner → bent-arm strength →
   accessories → flexibility. — CONSENSUS for the template; that the first
   exercise gains most is STRONG for dynamic lifts and INFERENCE for holds;
   that fatigue impairs skill learning is MODERATE in the lab. Never say this
   order prevents injury (see §1.7, item 18).
3. **Cap what runs at once:** at most one straight-arm push and one
   straight-arm pull progressing together; in the bent-arm pushing family one
   progresses and any other sits on maintenance; 2–4 skill-practice items;
   about five active skills in total. — CONSENSUS, and the copy should say the
   sources variously give two to four, five, and one or two.
4. **Pair push with pull:** planche with front lever; handstand with an
   L-sit/compression partner. Alternate them with roughly 90–120 s between
   when time is short, otherwise 3 minutes or more. — CONSENSUS ([L1]); rest
   lengths MODERATE by transfer (Grgic 2018).
5. **Frequency by level:** beginners full body 3×/week; then a two-way split —
   push/pull or straight-arm/bent-arm; each priority strength skill 2–3×/week
   (3 at most for a new planche); handstand balance 2–4×/week, more only once
   built up. — CONSENSUS; coaches disagree on daily handstands ([L7] vs
   [G2]). Frequency itself: ledger §3.
6. **Dose per straight-arm skill per session:** about 30–65 s of hold time in
   4–5 sets at 60–70% of max hold (Low) or 60 s at 50–80% (steady-state
   cycle). Coach examples run two or three such skills in a session, i.e.
   roughly 1.5–3 minutes of straight-arm holds, inside a session of about 90
   minutes at most. — CONSENSUS for per-skill dose (and it brackets the
   existing ~1 minute key-hold aggregate); the combined total is OPEN and must
   be labelled a planning heuristic.
7. **Priority and maintenance:** the priority skill goes first and on the hard
   day; a non-priority skill keeps its current progression on about one
   session a week and one or two sets. — CONSENSUS; MODERATE for dynamic
   strength; INFERENCE for holds; OPEN for balance.
8. **Easy weeks:** every 4–8 weeks halve volume, keep intensity; cut the
   isometrics first and keep handstand and support practice. — CONSENSUS;
   deloads remain convention (ledger §5).
9. **Budget by joint, not by skill.** Planche, front lever and flag draw on
   one elbow/shoulder straight-arm budget; planche, handstand, handstand
   push-up and 90° work draw on one wrist (hand-support) budget; handstand
   push-up, planche and 90° on one pushing-shoulder budget. A joint report
   from any skill should reach every skill that loads that joint, and the
   week-on-week pain rail should be per joint. Do not step up two skills in
   the same budget in the same week. — the grouping is CONSENSUS
   ([L3], [A1], [G1], [G2]); the budgets and the one-step-per-week rule are
   **design heuristics (INFERENCE)** and may describe load, never predict
   injury (ledger §6).
10. **Prerequisite order across skills:** a bent-arm base before straight-arm
    work; an L-sit before the tuck planche; solid basic pulling before the
    front lever; wall handstand push-ups before the flag; 90° push-ups arrive
    with free handstand push-ups and the planche, late. — CONSENSUS, each
    resting on one or two coaches ([M1], [B5], [FL1], [A2], [C1]).
11. **Model overlap as partial credit:** a set for one skill counts in part
    toward a related skill's weekly work. — STRONG that fractional counting
    fits dynamic-lift data best (Pelland 2026); INFERENCE for isometrics; any
    coefficient is the app's.
12. **Do not optimise the order of skills within the practice block.** Blocked
    or interleaved made almost no difference in applied settings. — STRONG
    (Czyż 2024).

## 1.7 Additions to "what the app must not claim"

Continuing the ledger's numbering.

15. Any number of concurrent skills (2–4, 5, one or two) as measured or
    optimal.
16. Any ceiling on straight-arm isometric time per session or week as
    evidence-based.
17. That pairing planche with front lever balances the shoulder in a way that
    prevents injury.
18. That skill practice before strength work, or as a warm-up, prevents
    injury. The one survey found in this pass that asked reported
    technique-as-warm-up *associated with more* injury; it cannot show cause
    either way.
19. That daily handstand practice is safe or recommended. Coaches disagree and
    wrist pain is common in every population surveyed.

## 2.6 Feature ideas athletes evidently value

Each is grounded in something opened; none is a claim about a competitor's
quality.

1. **A map of each skill with the next step and what unlocks it.** Why: it
   answers what to train next, the question several apps lead with. Seen:
   Calistree skill trees; Kinetic; Forma; CaliQuest (shows the unlock
   requirement); Fitloop progression trees; Calistack.
2. **Goal priorities with a visible cap and a rotation.** Why: coaches cap
   concurrency (§1.6 #3) and one product documents it. Seen: Movement Athlete
   (five skills, 2–4-week blocks, main vs side); Forma (pick goal skills);
   Fitloop's guide; a Calisteniapp review asking for more than one skill.
3. **Skills grouped by the kind of work** — hand balance, straight-arm,
   bent-arm push, pull. Why: it is how the coaches schedule (§1.6 #1). Seen:
   CaliQuest's six families; Merrick's bent-arm/straight-arm split; Statik's
   radar.
4. **Sessions per week and minutes per session stated per skill, up front.**
   Why: lets an athlete see whether three goals fit their week before
   committing. Seen: Bodyweight Warrior program cards; FitnessFAQs program
   pages.
5. **One calendar that holds several plans plus one-off sessions**, with drag
   to reschedule. Why: Cali Move rebuilt around it on user request. Seen:
   Cali Move 2.0; Bodyweight Warrior (combine programs); Heria Pro calendar.
6. **Placement that is shown, explained and overridable.** Why: silent level
   changes were confusing enough for a developer to say so publicly. Seen:
   Cali Move 2.0; Movement Athlete's manual step/reps/mastered controls;
   Bodyweight Warrior's assessment workouts.
7. **Change the progression in the moment** — easier or harder, before or
   during a set. Why: the commonest difficulty complaint is a plan that will
   not bend. Seen: Cali Move 2.0; Calisthenics Family (swap to an easier or
   harder variation); Fitloop; requested in a Calistree review (configurable
   progression rate and rest).
8. **A return-after-a-break choice** — continue, ease back in, or start over.
   Why: avoids both an outdated schedule and an accidental reset. Seen: Cali
   Move 2.0 (after three weeks away).
9. **A standards table per skill with today's target and the bar for the next
   step** — labelled as convention. Why: athletes want to know where they
   stand. Seen: Tom Merrick's strength levels; Cali Move's level pyramid;
   Calisthenics Family's target-and-milestone; Statik's readiness thresholds.
   (Fitloop's guide and GMB's planche page also print timelines — the ledger
   forbids those.)
10. **Prerequisite checks before a skill opens**, stated as coaching
    convention. Seen: Fitloop's guide (pulling base and hollow hold before the
    front lever); Antranik (wall handstand push-ups before the flag);
    Bodyweight Warrior (pancake before press or planche).
11. **A push/pull/straight-arm balance view of the week.** Why: it makes Low's
    balance rule visible and is a popular analytic. Seen: Statik's radar;
    muscle maps in Thenx, Calisteniapp, Madbarz; Calistree's per-joint levels.
12. **Per-side logging for one-sided skills** (the flag). Why: the asymmetry
    is large and coaches insist on both sides. Seen: Antranik's flag tutorial.
    No app opened advertises it; Statik's per-set grip type is the nearest.
13. **Hold cues that can be heard and do not stop the athlete's music.** Why:
    a recurring complaint, and for holds the cue *is* the product. Seen:
    complaints against Movement Athlete, Calisteniapp, Freeletics, Madbarz;
    Calistree's hands-free audio as the positive case.
14. **A camera stopwatch that counts only the stable hold, with a stated
    tolerance mode, and a clip saved with the time on it.** Why: it is the
    leading promise of the new handstand apps, and the one CaliPro review with
    detail is about timing starting at the wrong moment. Seen: Handstand
